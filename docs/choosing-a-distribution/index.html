    Choosing a distribution¶

    This page is about deciding, before you train anything, whether your counts need the zero-inflated distribution or whether the plain one is enough. It covers the call that makes the decision, what the numbers mean, where it refuses to answer.

    The question¶

    PREFFECT can fit counts with either of two distributions. The negative binomial says a gene has an average level and a spread around it wider than pure counting noise. The zero-inflated negative binomial is the same thing plus a separate probability that a count is zero for a reason unrelated to expression level, such as the transcript never being captured.

    Adding that probability always fits the data at least as well, because it can be set to nothing and recover the plain distribution. So fitting better is not a reason to use it. The question is whether it fits enough better to be worth the extra parameter, and the Akaike information criterion is the usual way to answer it. The criterion is the fitted log-likelihood penalised by the number of parameters, and the smaller value wins.

    The PREFFECT paper asked exactly this question of fRNA-seq counts and found that the plain distribution is usually enough, which is why Likelihood defaults to it. The code here is the estimator that produced that answer, ported so the evidence can be reproduced and asked again of a new cohort.

    One gene¶

    compare_fits takes one gene's counts across samples and returns both fits with the verdict.

    import numpy as np

from preffect.zinb_fit import compare_fits

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)


def draw(mean, dispersion, zero_probability, n=400):
    counts = rng.negative_binomial(
        dispersion, dispersion / (dispersion + mean), size=n
    ).astype(float)
    counts[rng.random(n) < zero_probability] = 0.0
    return counts


plain = draw(mean=30, dispersion=3, zero_probability=0.0)
comparison = compare_fits(plain)

    >>> comparison.preferred
'NB'
>>> round(comparison.aic_difference, 2)
-1.33
>>> comparison.negative_binomial
NegativeBinomialFit(mean=30.97750020843003, dispersion=2.754773927681657,
                    log_likelihood=-1701.9800050052718, aic=3407.9600100105436,
                    dispersion_at_bound=False)

    The same call on counts that really do have extra zeros:

    inflated = draw(mean=30, dispersion=3, zero_probability=0.4)
comparison = compare_fits(inflated)

    >>> comparison.preferred, round(comparison.aic_difference, 1)
('ZINB', 306.5)
>>> comparison.zero_inflated
ZeroInflatedFit(mean=30.300878641209515, dispersion=3.418954422277129,
                zero_probability=0.38475435143728937,
                log_likelihood=-1290.230705904805, aic=2586.46141180961,
                dispersion_at_bound=False, starts_converged=8)
>>> comparison.zero_inflated.observed_mean
18.642483731630936

    Reading the numbers¶

    Four of the reported quantities need saying out loud, because three of them are easy to read as something they are not.

    • aic_difference is the plain fit's criterion minus the zero-inflated one's, so positive means the extra zeros earned their parameter. A difference under 2 means the two fit about equally well. Above 10 the evidence is usually called strong.
    • mean is the mean of the negative binomial component, not of the distribution. Above, the component's mean is 30.3 while the counts themselves average 18.6, because two counts in five were forced to zero. observed_mean is the second number.
    • dispersion is PREFFECT's theta, the inverse dispersion. Large means close to Poisson, small means heavily overdispersed, and the variance is mean + mean**2 / dispersion. A textbook that calls the dispersion alpha means 1 / theta.
    • dispersion_at_bound means the counts are at least as tight as a Poisson, so the dispersion is not identified and what is reported is the largest value the fit allows. This is common on genes with almost no counts and on anything close to constant.

    A whole matrix¶

    compare_fits_over_columns runs the same comparison down every column of a counts matrix, which is one gene each in a samples-by-genes matrix, and returns a table with one row per gene.

    from preffect.zinb_fit import compare_fits_over_columns

matrix = np.column_stack(
    [plain, inflated, draw(5, 1, 0.0), draw(80, 6, 0.6)]
)
table = compare_fits_over_columns(
    matrix, names=["GENE_A", "GENE_B", "GENE_C", "GENE_D"]
)

    >>> table[["name", "zero_fraction", "preferred",
...        "aic_difference", "zinb_zero_probability"]].round(3)
     name  zero_fraction preferred  aic_difference  zinb_zero_probability
0  GENE_A          0.002        NB          -1.332                  0.002
1  GENE_B          0.385      ZINB         306.510                  0.385
2  GENE_C          0.188        NB          -1.364                  0.037
3  GENE_D          0.565      ZINB         429.242                  0.565

    GENE_C is the row worth looking at. Nearly a fifth of its counts are zero and the plain distribution still wins, because a gene with a mean of 5 and a wide spread produces that many zeros on its own. A high proportion of zeros is not evidence of zero inflation by itself, which is the whole reason for fitting rather than counting.

    A DataFrame brings its own column names, so names can be left out when you pass one.

    Where it refuses to answer¶

    Three situations raise rather than returning a number, because a number would be a guess.

    What happened What is raised Why
    The values are not whole, non-negative, finite numbers DataError A count distribution has no probability to give them. Round the counts yourself if that is what you mean, as the paper's notebooks did.
    Every count is zero FitError Any mean fits equally well as long as the extra-zero probability is one, so nothing is identified.
    No search converged FitError There is no fit to report.

    In a sweep over thousands of genes, stopping at the first gene that cannot be fitted is the default, because a sweep that quietly drops genes turns a claim about a cohort into a claim about whichever subset happened to fit. Pass on_failure="record" to carry on instead; the reason goes in the row's problem column and its numbers are left missing.

    How long it takes¶

    Fitting both distributions to one gene across 220 samples takes about 0.18 seconds on one thread. So 2,000 genes take six minutes and a 19,000-gene cohort takes about an hour. Nothing here is on a training path, so it has not been made faster than that.

    If you are sweeping a whole cohort, the reason to pass on_failure="record" is that an hour's work should not be thrown away by one gene. Read the problem column afterwards and decide what to do about the rows that have one.

    Reproducing the paper's comparison¶

    The reference values are in tests/data/version_0_1_zinb_fits.json: nine count vectors with the fits the paper's notebooks compared against. tests/test_zinb_fit.py checks the estimator against them.
